Project Overview

Europa is one of the most scientifically compelling destinations in the solar system, with a global subsurface ocean that makes it a primary target for astrobiology. While orbital and flyby missions can study Europa remotely, a landing mission enables in-situ measurement of surface material that may be indicative of the ocean below. Safe landing on Europa presents a challenging control problem driven by autonomous navigation requirements, stringent terminal constraints, limited propellant, and uncertain initial conditions for the approach phase.

This project developed a fuel-optimal powered descent guidance framework for a Europa lander, formulated as a convex Second-Order Cone Program (SOCP) with lossless convexification of the nonlinear thrust magnitude constraints following the approach of Açıkmeşe and Ploen. The full EDL problem was decomposed into two coupled phases — Entry and Descent/Landing — connected through a handoff state. The Entry phase was used to derive realistic initial conditions for the Descent/Landing phase, which is the primary focus of this work. I was responsible for the initial 3DOF linearized setup, the reference minimum-fuel convex trajectory, the initial MPC implementation, and the open-loop introduction of Brownian motion disturbances; Owen Willis developed the nonlinear RK4 truth model and led the Monte Carlo robustness analysis.

Approach

Step 1 — Minimum-Time Feasibility & Full-Horizon Reference Trajectory

The descent problem was modeled using 3DOF translational dynamics in a local Cartesian frame fixed to the Europa landing site, with a seven-state formulation: three position components, three velocity components, and log-mass. Thrust was losslessly convexified by introducing an auxiliary magnitude variable, converting the nonconvex thrust constraint into a second-order cone constraint. A bisection search over the number of timesteps first identified the minimum feasible descent duration. With that horizon fixed at N = 45 steps (90 seconds), a minimum-fuel SOCP was then solved over the full horizon, consuming 109.7 kg of propellant and producing the nominal reference trajectory used by the MPC controller.

Step 2 — Receding-Horizon MPC (Closed-Loop Descent)

The open-loop reference trajectory was extended into a closed-loop framework using receding-horizon Model Predictive Control. At each timestep k, a finite-horizon SOCP was solved over a window of Nh = min(25, Nrem) steps using the current true lander state as the initial condition. The cost function combined minimum-fuel (−ηN) with quadratic reference tracking weighted by a diagonal matrix Q, balancing propellant efficiency against trajectory fidelity. Only the first control action was applied, after which the true state was propagated forward one step using a nonlinear RK4 integrator with altitude-dependent Europa gravity — separating the convex prediction model from the higher-fidelity truth model. This receding-horizon structure ensured that any deviation introduced by nonlinear dynamics or handoff uncertainty was absorbed at the next solve step.

Step 3 — Robustness Analysis (Monte Carlo)

Two robustness studies evaluated controller performance under uncertainty. In the deterministic study, fixed ±100 m position and ±5 m/s velocity perturbations were applied one-at-a-time to the handoff state; the MPC controller successfully converged to a feasible landing in all cases, with the largest propellant penalties (~1 kg) arising from velocity perturbations in vx and vz. The Gaussian Monte Carlo study (20 trials, σpos = 100 m, σvel = 5 m/s) confirmed that the closed-loop MPC reduced terminal position scatter by approximately two orders of magnitude relative to the open-loop baseline under equivalent disturbance levels. A correlation heatmap further showed that initial downrange velocity error (Δvx,0) was the dominant contributor to both propellant cost and terminal position accuracy.

Results & Discussion

The minimum-fuel full-horizon solution converged at N = 45 timesteps (90 seconds), consuming 109.7 kg of propellant and achieving a touchdown mass of 409.1 kg at 2.001 m/s. Terminal position errors were 0.54 m in x and 0.12 m in y, well within the soft-landing constraint bounds. The thrust magnitude remained near saturation for most of the descent, consistent with a vehicle operating near its performance limit at the minimum feasible descent time.

The closed-loop MPC controller achieved a terminal lateral position error of 1.68 m and satisfied all velocity constraints, with an average SOCP solve time of 899 ms per step — within the assumed onboard compute budget for a 2-second integration interval. Tracking error analysis showed that the largest deviations occurred in the vertical direction, where altitude-dependent gravity introduced the primary nonlinear mismatch between the constant-gravity optimizer model and the truth propagator. The tracking weight matrix Q provided a direct tuning mechanism: larger weights constrained maximum position error to ~3 m and velocity error to ~0.6 m/s, while smaller weights allowed more freedom for feasibility and disturbance rejection.

The open-loop baseline (Tasks 4–5) placed the majority of 50 Monte Carlo trials outside the 50 m position tolerance and produced lateral velocity scatter of ±1.5 m/s against a ±0.2 m/s constraint — directly motivating the closed-loop MPC architecture. The contrast is stark: removing feedback allows every perturbation to integrate freely across all remaining timesteps, while the MPC controller limits disturbance growth to a single 2-second interval before the next correction is applied.
